Turning an OpenClaw voice interface from research rig into a usable product

Project period · September 2026

After a semantic-grounding research path stalled on a training-level limit, Mark 4 rebuilt on the proven transport and iterated on turn-taking and pacing until the verdict changed from "not bad" to "really good."

  • Voice
  • Agent Architecture
  • WebRTC
  • Product

In brief

  • An earlier eight-stage attempt to give a duplex speech model exact live knowledge of the world hit a training-level wall, not a wiring problem, and was set aside.
  • Mark 4 built on the already-proven Mark 2 transport, treating voice as a product to iterate rather than a research target to perfect.
  • A strict “speak only checked answers, and prove pending work rather than claim it” policy fixed the model’s tendency to talk over itself or invent progress.
  • A pacing/thinking-cue design closed most of the awkward silence without adding a false “I’m working on it” that wasn’t true.
  • Across several days of measured stages, Phil’s live verdict moved from “not bad” to “really good,” with barge-in described as “natural and seamless.”

Setup, September 2026: an M4 Mac mini running macOS 26.4.1, with Tiggy on OpenClaw 2026.9.4. All speech handling — WebRTC capture and echo cancellation, voice-activity detection, local speech recognition and synthesis — runs locally on the same machine.

Voice work on Tiggy went through an earlier phase, called Mark 3. It tried to give a full-duplex speech model exact, live, grounded knowledge of a running assistant’s own internal state, not just its transcript.

After eight measured stages of targeted fixes, the team concluded the remaining gap was a property of how the speech model had been trained; wiring or prompting couldn’t fix it. That work was preserved as a record of what was tried, and a new, more product-shaped effort began: Mark 4.

Question

Given a voice transport that already worked, proven in Mark 2, what would it take to make talking to an agent feel like a product, not a demo? That transport covered WebRTC, echo cancellation, recognition and synthesis.

Setup

Mark 4 reused the Mark 2 transport rather than rebuilding it. That meant browser WebRTC capture and echo cancellation, local voice-activity detection, a local speech-recognition model, and a local speech-synthesis model, all on the same Mac mini.

It ran as an isolated service on its own ports and LaunchAgent, so Mark 2, kept running as fallback and comparison baseline, was never at risk. Tiggy was the default backend; Mark 2’s ability to switch agents by voice stayed documented but not yet reactivated.

The team explored, then set aside, a lighter-weight “voice-front” agent designed to answer instantly from local context and delegate anything unfamiliar. A structured benchmark compared that split design against sending everything to the full authoritative agent.

The full-agent route gave a correct or useful answer for a much larger share of real turns. A parallel-speculation variant of the lighter design produced no completed useful text in the fast lane across eighteen realistic test turns. The full, single-brain approach was kept.

What we tried

Turn integrity first. Early live testing exposed a basic bug. A person pausing mid-sentence could be treated as two separate turns, or a same-turn correction could arrive after the assistant had already started answering the earlier, wrong version. Mark 4 added an explicit turn-completion and turn-cancellation contract: a paused, resumed sentence produces exactly one user turn and one reply, and a same-turn revision cleanly cancels the predecessor before it’s spoken.

An honest thinking cue, not a fake one. The first instinct for hiding response latency was filler (“let me check that…”). The actual design was narrower: speak real, useful text the moment it’s available.

If there’s no answer after roughly a second, play a quiet audio cue rather than words, repeating it only after another long silence if work is still pending. Only speak a words-based progress update after several more seconds and an observed, real milestone, never a generic “still working” with nothing behind it.

Cancel any pending cue instantly the moment a real answer or the person’s own speech arrives. This was deliberately conservative: better to say less than say something untrue about progress.

Pre-tool commentary, measured, rolled back, then fixed properly. Wiring the assistant’s own brief, factual description of what it was about to do into the speech queue produced a large improvement. Across ten real tool-using turns, the time from decision to useful audio dropped from roughly 2.6 seconds to roughly 0.3 seconds at the median.

The change was initially rolled back after an old test fixture appeared to fail, then correctly re-deployed once the failure was isolated to the fixture. Chasing that regression surfaced a separate bug: the streaming text used to detect a stable sentence could momentarily publish a truncated identifier or number before the real, complete one arrived.

The fix made a hard rule: any value the assistant can’t get wrong is only ever published from a finished, authoritative response, never an in-progress snapshot.

Making the reply feel controllable, not just fast. A later iteration exposed an adjustable speech rate (roughly 0.85x to 1.15x) in the interface. It also added Home Screen installability so the voice interface behaved like an ordinary iPhone app icon. It smoothed a measured but cosmetically distracting flicker between “speaking” and “thinking” states, without touching audio timing.

Bringing it off the home network. A further stage added authenticated remote access over the public internet, through an existing reverse proxy and a locked-down TURN relay server. This let the interface be used away from the home LAN. This was verified with real mobile-data connection tests.

Result

Each stage carried its own automated regression suite alongside the always-protected Mark 2 suite, and every stage report recorded both green. The most meaningful evidence was a sequence of unscripted live conversations. An early stage drew the flat verdict “not bad.” A later stage, after the turn-integrity and thinking-cue work, drew a stronger one:

“has passed the inflection point of being legitimately useful and enjoyable to use.”

After the pacing and speed-control refinement, a five-minute phone conversation, plus two Home Screen relaunch checks, produced the verdict “really good.”

A live spoken interruption stopped the assistant’s own audio in well under a second, described as “natural and seamless” and “brilliant.”

Objective latency work continued in parallel. The largest measured single-turn win came not from a clever architecture change but from keeping one full agent warm with fast inference enabled.

Targeted improvements to how quickly a decision translated into audible commentary added to that. The 2.6-to-0.3-second improvement above was the clearest example of an engineering fix producing a directly felt experience change.

What we would do next

The strongest deferred item is reintroducing Mark 2’s agent-switching Operator commands into the Mark 4 product line. That would make the same polished voice experience available for more than one backend agent.

A second is widening the tested speech-rate range only with further naturalness and interruption evidence, since the team was deliberately conservative about raising it without proof. A third is investigating why iOS repeatedly re-prompts for microphone permission even behind a stable HTTPS origin, a platform behaviour a certificate alone doesn’t appear to fully solve.
