Building an iPhone voice interface for an OpenClaw agent

Project period · September 2026

A LAN-only WebRTC experiment turned an iPhone into the ears and voice of a local AI assistant—without replacing OpenClaw as the brain.

  • Tiggy
  • Voice
  • OpenClaw
  • Local AI
  • WebRTC

In brief

  • An iPhone browser became Tiggy’s microphone and speaker over LAN-only WebRTC; OpenClaw remained the brain.
  • Nemotron Speech ASR 0.6B handled transcription and Kokoro 82M generated a British female voice locally.
  • Final transcription took roughly 0.24–0.60 seconds; warm text-to-speech produced first audio in 0.11–0.13 seconds.
  • A live kitchen conversation—not just synthetic tests—proved the prototype usable.
  • Interruption, tool-time steering and rejection of tiny false utterances remain experimental.

The question

Could an existing OpenClaw agent hold a natural hands-free conversation through an iPhone without becoming a separate assistant? The agent is called Tiggy; her tools, durable memory, model routing and session context already lived in OpenClaw 2026.9 on a Mac mini. The experimental voice layer should supply ears and a mouth, not a second identity or control plane.

The Mac mini had no useful microphone. An iPhone already had good microphones, echo cancellation and WebRTC-capable Safari.

The path was:

iPhone Safari → WebRTC → speech detection → local transcription → OpenClaw → streamed response → local speech synthesis → iPhone

Everything stayed on the home network. No router port, public service, database or production-model change was required.

The local speech loop

NVIDIA Nemotron Speech ASR 0.6B transcribed speech; Kokoro 82M spoke replies using the British female bf_emma voice. Together they used about 2 GB of memory.

Safari supplied capture-side echo cancellation. Server-side voice detection plus an adjustable energy gate rejected background noise. As OpenClaw returned stable sentences, Kokoro began speaking before the full answer existed. Listening continued during playback so new speech could cancel queued audio.

Results

Laboratory checks found:

  • final transcription in roughly 0.24–0.60 seconds;
  • warm first speech audio in 0.11–0.13 seconds;
  • about 71 dB echo reduction with no false detections in synthetic echo tests;
  • a correct transcript and non-silent reply in an injected end-to-end WebRTC test running under the persistent macOS service.

The decisive test happened in the kitchen. Phil opened Safari, set the noise threshold to about −37 dBFS and held a natural conversation while the phone showed recognised text, audio level and speaking state.

Teaching it “Tiggy”

The recogniser heard “Tiggy” as “Ticky”. Nemotron’s MLX implementation had no genuine hotword input, so the fix used a narrow decoder bias only after a plausible near-miss plus a conservative fallback for a few standalone spellings.

The recorded phrase changed to “Tiggy, are you there?” while “Tina”, “tiger” and “tricky” stayed unchanged. Phil then confirmed the live result was “much better”.

Still a prototype

Streaming removes delay after generation starts; it cannot hide a slow first token. The next tests are deliberate interruption while Tiggy speaks, steering during tool use, better rejection of tiny utterances and clearer latency telemetry.

Remote mobile-data access can wait. The experiment answered the useful question: an iPhone can be a natural local voice terminal while Tiggy remains Tiggy.
