Building a low-cost Frigate camera estate with Thingino

Project period · July 2026

Scaling a mixed Thingino, Tapo and Reolink camera estate to fourteen units under one Frigate instance stayed affordable once onboarding, config backup and PTZ control were made repeatable.

  • Frigate
  • Cameras
  • Home Lab
  • Home Assistant

In brief

  • A camera estate grew from a handful of cheap Thingino cameras to fourteen mixed units under one Frigate instance, with no subscription.
  • A near-identical config pattern for each Thingino camera made adding new units mostly mechanical, backed up through Frigate’s own config API.
  • A brief CPU-saturation incident, caused by silent force-transcoding, was fixed the same way described in the direct-RTSP note.
  • Frigate’s own pan/tilt controls worked for both Thingino PTZ units and a motorized Tapo TC70, making a separate ONVIF integration optional.
  • The same onboarding pattern that scaled the estate to fourteen cameras is directly reusable for any future addition.

Question

Consumer NVR subscriptions and proprietary camera ecosystems are expensive and lock you into a vendor’s app. The alternative here combines Frigate, a self-hosted, Coral-accelerated open-source NVR, with a growing mix of cheap cameras. These include Cinnado/Thingino D1-chipset units running open firmware, plus consumer Tapo and Reolink cameras already in use around the house.

Once the estate had grown past a handful of cameras, the question was whether a mixed, incrementally-grown estate could keep scaling reliably. That meant onboarding new units, surviving config changes, and supporting PTZ hardware from different vendors, without a subscription or constant manual firefighting.

Setup

Frigate ran on the home Unraid server with a Coral USB accelerator for object detection, managed through Frigate’s own config-save-and-restart API. Three camera families made up the estate.

Cinnado/Thingino PTZ units, identified over the LAN by their thingino HTTP title and RTSP port 554, made up the largest group. Fixed and motorized Tapo units (TC60/TC70/C520WS) connected via ONVIF port 2020, alongside a single Reolink PoE doorbell.

Each camera got a go2rtc main/sub stream pair — detection from the lighter substream, recording from the full main stream — plus camera-group membership for the dashboard. By this stage the estate had grown to fourteen cameras across the three families.

What we tried

A repeatable per-camera onboarding pattern. Adding a new Thingino camera became close to mechanical. Scan the expected IP range for the device’s mDNS name and open ports.

Back up the live config, generate a candidate config mirroring the existing Thingino pattern, apply it through the API, then verify. Verification checked that the config listed the new camera, the snapshot endpoint returned a real JPEG, and camera/detection FPS settled at the expected rate. Every change had a diff, a backup, and a functional check, not just a look at the preview.

Discovering PTZ control was already solved. A new Tapo TC70 with motorized pan/tilt suggested a need for Home Assistant’s ONVIF integration to get dashboard PTZ buttons. It turned out Frigate’s own live-view arrow controls already drove pan/tilt on both the TC70 and the existing Thingino PTZ units, with no extra integration needed. HA ONVIF stayed an optional convenience layer rather than the assumed primary control path.

A CPU incident, resolved by an already-documented fix. As the estate grew past a dozen cameras, several Thingino stream aliases were silently force-transcoded through software libx264 instead of direct RTSP. Host CPU hit roughly 98-100%.

The diagnosis and fix are covered in full in Direct RTSP versus transcoding. The fix here was the same pattern applied to newly onboarded cameras that had inherited the forced-ffmpeg alias by copy-paste from an older config.

Interoperability across three PTZ implementations. With Thingino PTZ units, a Tapo TC70, and ONVIF-capable hardware side by side, Frigate’s live-view arrow controls drove pan/tilt identically across all three. No per-vendor glue code was needed. Adding a differently-branded PTZ camera needed no new integration path, just the same onboarding pattern applied again.

Result

Fourteen cameras spanning Thingino, Tapo and Reolink hardware run under one Frigate instance at roughly 5 fps camera/process/detection rate each, with Coral inference typically single-digit milliseconds. Retention was tuned deliberately (continuous 1→2 days, motion 3→6 days) after confirming ample headroom on the media share. Scaling three times over, from a handful of cameras to fourteen across three vendors and two PTZ implementations, never required abandoning the original onboarding pattern.

What we would do next

The onboarding pattern proven here — network scan, config backup, candidate diff, API apply, functional verification — is reusable for any future camera addition, regardless of vendor or PTZ capability.
