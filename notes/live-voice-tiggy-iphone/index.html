Giving Tiggy a live voice through an iPhone

A LAN-only WebRTC experiment turned an iPhone into the ears and voice of a local AI assistant—without replacing OpenClaw as the brain.

  • Tiggy
  • Voice
  • OpenClaw
  • Local AI
  • WebRTC

The question

Could Tiggy hold a natural, hands-free conversation without turning into a completely separate voice assistant?

The important constraint was that OpenClaw should remain the brain. Its tools, memory, model routing and ongoing session context all needed to survive. The voice layer should behave more like a new pair of ears and a mouth than a new personality.

Why the iPhone mattered

The Mac mini running Tiggy has speakers but no usable microphone. An iPhone already has excellent microphones, echo cancellation and a browser capable of WebRTC, so it became the audio front end.

The resulting path was:

iPhone Safari → WebRTC → speech detection → local transcription → OpenClaw → streamed response → local speech synthesis → WebRTC → iPhone speaker

Everything stayed on the home network. The prototype did not require a router port, public service, database or change to Tiggy’s production model and failover configuration.

The local speech stack

Two small Apple-Silicon-friendly models run on demand:

  • NVIDIA Nemotron Speech ASR 0.6B transcribes Phil’s speech locally.
  • Kokoro 82M generates Tiggy’s reply with the British female bf_emma voice.

Safari performs capture-side acoustic echo cancellation. The server adds WebRTC voice activity detection and an adjustable energy gate so kitchen noise does not constantly manufacture conversations.

When OpenClaw starts returning text, the voice layer does not wait for the entire answer. It collects stable sentences and sends them to Kokoro as they arrive. Listening continues during playback, so detected speech can cancel queued audio before the replacement utterance has even finished transcribing.

What worked

The laboratory measurements were encouraging:

  • Typical final transcription took roughly 0.24–0.60 seconds.
  • Warm text-to-speech produced first audio in roughly 0.11–0.13 seconds.
  • The two resident speech models used about 2 GB of memory together.
  • Synthetic echo tests produced no false speech detections and about 71 dB of echo reduction.
  • An injected end-to-end WebRTC test returned a correct transcript and non-silent reply audio while running under its persistent macOS service.

But the meaningful test happened in the kitchen. Phil opened the page in Safari, adjusted the noise threshold to about −37 dBFS, and held a natural conversation with Tiggy. Live status, recognised text, audio level and speaking state were all visible on the phone.

That turned this from a benchmark into a user-proven prototype.

Teaching it the name “Tiggy”

The recogniser initially heard “Tiggy” as “Ticky”. Nemotron’s MLX implementation has no genuine hotword or contextual-vocabulary input, so pretending otherwise would have produced a brittle fix.

Instead, the live recogniser now uses British English plus a narrow decoder bias that activates only after the audio has already entered a plausible near-miss path. A conservative post-processing fallback handles a few standalone spellings.

Phil’s recorded phrase changed from “Ticky, are you there?” to “Tiggy, are you there?”, while control words such as “Tina”, “tiger” and “tricky” remained unchanged. Phil then tested the change live and called it “much better”.

What remains experimental

This is still a LAN-only spike, not a finished product. The next useful tests are deliberate interruption while Tiggy is speaking and steering while an OpenClaw tool is still running. Tiny false utterances also need better rejection, and the page needs clearer latency telemetry.

Model thinking time can still dominate a reply. Streaming speech removes the delay at the end of generation, but it cannot hide a slow first token.

Remote use over mobile data is a later problem. For now, the result is simpler and more valuable: the iPhone can act as a natural local voice terminal while Tiggy remains Tiggy.
