Constrained delegation to a local Hermes agent

Project period · September 2026

A local worker became useful through constrained commands, explicit authority and fail-closed task envelopes.

  • Agent Architecture
  • Hermes
  • Security
  • Delegation

In brief

  • We wanted a local worker to perform useful jobs without inheriting Tiggy’s authority.
  • Early adapters exposed one forced command, a bounded input and a durable result.
  • A small broker recorded principal, supervisor, worker, direction and permissions.
  • No destructive action, direct messaging, external write or home control was allowed by default.
  • If a task or transport was ambiguous, the worker had to stop or ask—not improvise.

Hermes Agent is an open-source agent runtime used here as a local worker beneath Tiggy, the supervising OpenClaw agent. Delegating to another agent creates two risks: the model may misunderstand the task, and the plumbing may accidentally give it more authority than intended. We designed for both by separating a worker’s capability from its authority.

The worker ran on a 4-vCPU, 8 GB Ubuntu 26.04 VM. Hermes moved from 0.16.0 during the original June setup to 0.18.0 by the September review; the delegation design deliberately avoided depending on one model or provider version.

Start with a forced path

The first live Hermes jobs did not accept arbitrary shell text. A wrapper selected the operation, gathered known inputs, invoked one worker path and saved the result. Video review, project watching and annual-date lookahead each acquired a separate adapter.

This was less flexible than handing over a terminal. It was also inspectable. Inputs and outputs had stable locations; timeouts were visible; a partial result could not silently become a message or calendar write.

Constrain the worker’s authority

Our experimental broker recorded who originated a task, who received it, its direction and its permissions. Phil was the principal, Tiggy the supervising coordinator and Hermes a subordinate worker. A delegate flowed down that hierarchy; a worker’s request flowed up as a proposal requiring judgement.

Default task permissions excluded:

  • destructive commands;
  • direct messages to Phil;
  • email, calendar or other external writes;
  • home-infrastructure control.

The broker used HTTP, server-sent events and SQLite because the first question was the schema, not the transport. NATS remained a possible event fabric once the shape had earned permanence.

Fail closed at every seam

A worker could claim only tasks addressed to its identity and capabilities. The adapter checked the task direction and permission set before invoking Hermes. Outputs returned to the broker as reports; they were never interpreted as new instructions for Tiggy.

This last rule prevented a subtle authority inversion. If Hermes said “please fetch this URL” or “send this message”, that text was evidence from a worker, not permission from Phil. Tiggy still had to decide whether the follow-up was safe and in scope.

Result

Hermes gained useful work while its blast radius stayed small. The design did not assume the local model was malicious; it assumed that capable software eventually meets a malformed prompt, stale state or surprising output.

The reusable lesson is to delegate objectives, inputs and narrow capabilities together. Do not delegate your identity.
